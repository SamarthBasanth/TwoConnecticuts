Two Connecticuts:
the inequity written into
K-12 public school funding.

Picture two children waking up on the same morning. They pack the same backpack, share the same ambitions, and walk into schools twenty minutes apart. One has a counselor. One does not. One has updated textbooks. One does not. The distance between adequacy and deprivation in Connecticut is not measured in miles but in zip codes.

Chapter 01 · The Opening Scene

Twenty minutes. Two different educations.

The same backpack. The same ambition. The property values surrounding their school determine their access, not their potential.

We have watched this disparity grow for decades and called it a funding formula. Consider what it means in practice: a student who cannot access a school counselor during a mental health crisis, a classroom that cannot afford a debate or science bowl team, a district that loses its most experienced teacher to neighboring towns that offer salaries their tax base cannot compete with.

The opening scene of this piece is set deliberately in South Windsor. As a student who grew up there, I did not understand for years that my science labs, my counselors, my advanced courses, and my extracurriculars were not standard issue. They were a zip-code outcome. South Windsor sits roughly twenty minutes from Hartford by car, yet per-pupil spending in the two districts differs by nearly $3,000. That specific comparison is the clearest lens I have for a statewide pattern: the town I live in and the town next to it are supposed to be part of the same Connecticut.

When local property tax revenue is the primary funding mechanism, a district's wealth and its students' needs must move in opposite directions, by design. That is not an inevitability. It is a policy choice.

Chapter 02 · The Mechanism

Decentralized revenue produces stratified access.

Connecticut's 169 towns each fund their schools primarily from local property taxes. Home values run beneath everything: the invisible architecture of opportunity.

The inequity embedded in the American public school funding system is the predictable consequence of a decentralized revenue system where school funding is tied to the property wealth of the surrounding community. Glover and Levačić, in their comparative analysis published by UCL Press, demonstrate that this outcome is neither uniquely American nor surprising.

In regions with weak tax bases, fiscal equalization is not feasible, leaving school resourcing to local communities with consequent inadequacy and inequality. Their examination of funding systems across Pakistan, Luxembourg, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Kenya confirms that decentralized finance reliably produces stratified educational access. The United States is not an anomaly. It is a case study in a well-documented global pattern.

Chapter 03 · Vertical Equity

Equal dollars do not create equal opportunity.

Vesely and Crampton establish that vertical equity demands differential treatment of unequals. Students with greater needs must receive proportionally greater resources. We have never set that standard into practice.

A student in poverty requires wraparound social services. An English language learner requires specialized instruction. A student with disabilities requires intensive support. These services cost significantly more, meaning high-need districts require more per pupil than low-need districts just to reach the same educational standard.

Weighted student funding formulas are the most consistently effective distribution instrument identified across national contexts, specifically because they decouple resource allocation from geography and anchor it to individual student need. The framework is not missing. The commitment to executing it with fidelity is.

Chapter 04 · The Evidence

We do not have to guess whether this works.

The evidence is consistent over time. Sustained equitable funding for low-income students produces measurable, generational returns across education, earnings, and poverty reduction.

States that have applied meaningful weights to poverty, disability, and English language learner status have produced measurably more equitable distributions than those relying on categorical grants or foundation aid alone. We already have the formula. What we lack is the will to fund it.

Acknowledging need in policy language without substantively funding it produces no measurable change. Vesely & Crampton, Journal of Education Finance

Three decades of school finance litigation and legislative revision have also demonstrated a harder lesson: well-intentioned redistribution built around income metrics alone can reproduce existing inequalities in reconfigured form. A September 2025 study of forty states found that reforms narrowed income-based funding gaps but simultaneously widened racial ones, with districts serving the highest percentages of Black students losing $900 per pupil compared to wealthier districts. The failure was not in applying equity. It was applying it along only one axis. Our formulas must account for race and poverty simultaneously, not treat one as a stand-in for the other.

So what would a solution actually look like?

Four levers. Turned together, they change the math.

The research points to the same four structural reforms across decades of literature and across national contexts. No single one closes the gap. Applied together, they redefine the rules of the game. This is not aspirational language. Each of these levers already exists, somewhere, in statute. What separates Connecticut from a fairer model is sequencing them into a single formula and funding it.

An adequacy foundation sets the floor. Need-based weights acknowledge that a child in poverty, a newcomer learning English, and a student with an IEP do not cost the same to educate, and the formula says so in dollars. Wealth equalization reverses the compounding advantage of a high property tax base. Dual-axis targeting prevents reforms from solving one inequity while deepening another. That is the full model. It is designed, funded, and measured together.

None of this requires a new study. The frameworks have existed long enough. What remains is the work of writing them into law, indexing them to current data rather than frozen baselines, and committing to multi-year funding guarantees so districts can plan beyond a single fiscal year. That is the difference between a policy conversation and a policy outcome.

Chapter 05 · The Closing

Somewhere in New Britain tonight, a student is doing her algebra II homework.

Her district has already decided what she is worth, not by her questions or her effort, but by five digits she never chose.

She did not choose her ZIP code. She did not choose the funding tied to it or the opportunities it limits. She did not choose to be left behind. Yet she will live with the consequences.

We are still debating. She is already in the classroom, waiting for a system that has moved on without her.

The window to act is closing. Fund it before the cost becomes hers to carry.

Chapter 06 · Act

Three people. Three actions.

The research has traveled far enough. The frameworks have existed long enough. This is what acting on the evidence looks like.

Two Connecticuts. One solvable problem.
